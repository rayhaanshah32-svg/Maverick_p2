Reading is one of the most complex cognitive tasks humans perform. It requires the simultaneous coordination of eye movements, working memory, language processing, and attention — all happening in a fraction of a second. For people with dyslexia, this process is significantly harder, not because of intelligence, but because of how their brain processes text.

Eye tracking technology offers a window into the reading process that was previously only available to researchers in lab settings. Today, webcam-based systems like WebGazer can approximate gaze position with enough accuracy to track which line of text a person is reading — without any special hardware, just a standard laptop camera.

The Adaptive Reader uses this signal to detect when a reader is struggling — revisiting the same line repeatedly, reading unusually slowly, or losing their place — and automatically adjusts the display: larger fonts, wider spacing, highlighted focus lines, and simplified text when needed. All of this happens in real time, invisibly.

The key insight behind this system is that gaze tracking does not need to be word-perfect to be useful. Knowing which line a person is on is enough to provide meaningful, contextual support. This approach is both technically achievable with today's webcam hardware and meaningfully impactful for readers who need it most.
